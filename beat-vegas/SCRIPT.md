# Guy Wonders Why: "He Beat Vegas With a Computer in His Shoe"
Draft v1 · target 10–11 min (8+ min allows mid-roll ads) · about 1,450 narration words at a natural pace

## Why this one
- **RPM:** it starts as a science story (math, physics, the first wearable computer) and ends on Wall Street, so finance advertisers bid on it.
- **Shock:** it's all true and sounds impossible. A 1961 computer hidden in a shoe beat roulette.
- **Twist:** the payoff is hidden until the last 90 seconds. The man who beat every system caught Bernie Madoff 17 years before the world did.

## COLD OPEN (0:00–0:30): pattern interrupt, promise, stakes
*Black. A roulette ball rattling. Close on a shoe: a big toe presses down, click. A soft musical tone.*

> Las Vegas, 1961. The ball is still spinning, and this man already knows where it's going to land.
>
> Not luck. Not a rigged wheel. He's wearing a computer. Twelve transistors, the size of a cigarette pack, wired to switches in his shoes and a speaker in his ear.

*The ball drops. Chips slide across.*

> The house normally has a five percent edge on roulette. This machine gave him **forty-four.**
>
> Within a few years, the casinos would ban him, and one of them, he believed, would try to stop him for good. Then he'd walk away from Vegas for a much bigger table. And thirty years after this night, he'd look at the most trusted investor in America… and see what no regulator would see for seventeen more years.

## ACT 1: THE QUESTION (0:30–2:55)
> His name was Edward Thorp, and he started as a physics student in 1950s Los Angeles with a question nobody at the casinos wanted asked: **is roulette really random?**
>
> A roulette wheel isn't magic. It's a ball and a wheel, obeying the same laws as a planet in orbit. If you know how fast the ball is going and where it is, in principle you can predict where it slows down.

*Physics diagram: ball speed, friction, the decay curve.* (L1 progresses)

> By 1960 Thorp was teaching math at MIT, and he took the idea to the one person who might take it seriously: Claude Shannon, the man who invented information theory, the math behind every text, call and file you've ever sent.
>
> Shannon loved it. In his basement they bought a real casino wheel, filmed it, timed it, and built the thing.

*Blueprint-style breakdown of the device: toe switches, the 12-transistor box, the earpiece.*

> One player clicks a toe switch as the ball passes a mark, then again one lap later. The computer turns that into a prediction and plays one of eight tones in his ear, one for each slice of the wheel. **(L1 answered)** He doesn't need to know the number, just which eighth of the wheel. That alone is worth forty-four percent.
>
> On paper, it was a license to print money. But the machine had one flaw. **(L2 planted)**

## ACT 2: THE TEST, AND THE REVERSAL (2:55–4:00)
> In the summer of 1961 the Thorps and the Shannons went to Las Vegas. Claude stood by the wheel pretending to jot down numbers like any hopeful gambler. The predictions came in just as they had in the lab.

*Tension build: tone, click, win.*

> And then the flaw showed up. The wires to the earpiece were hair-thin, so they could hide under skin-colored paint and hair, and they kept breaking. **(L2 answered: midpoint reversal)** A computer in 1961 couldn't be both invisible and tough. They never bet big.
>
> The machine failed. **But the math didn't.** And Thorp had a second weapon.

## ACT 3: THE WAR WITH THE CASINOS (4:00–6:40)
> Blackjack. Casinos assumed it was pure chance, and it isn't, because blackjack has memory. Every card that's dealt is gone until the shuffle. When lots of small cards are gone, the rest of the deck quietly tilts toward the player.

*Deck visual: cards leaving, and the odds bar sliding from house-green to player-gold.*

> Thorp worked it out on one of MIT's early computers. Then Shannon handed him a second secret, a formula from Bell Labs called the **Kelly criterion**, which tells you exactly how much to bet when the odds are on your side. Too little and you waste the edge. Too much and one bad run wipes you out.
>
> A professional gambler named Manny Kimmel put up ten thousand dollars to test it. In Reno, over a single weekend, Thorp turned it into twenty-one thousand.
>
> In 1962 he published everything in a book called ***Beat the Dealer.*** It became a bestseller. Suddenly thousands of people were counting cards.
>
> The casinos fought back. They changed the rules and added more decks. They barred him. He started wearing disguises: glasses, a beard.

*Beat. The music drops out.*

> And then, by his own account, it got darker. At one casino, his coffee tasted strange. His pupils dilated and he couldn't count. A few nights later, driving home down a steep mountain road, his accelerator jammed to the floor. He got the car down to a stop, barely. A mechanic later said the linkage had been tampered with. **(L3 answered)**
>
> Nothing was ever proven. Thorp himself never accused anyone. He just said: this is what happened to me.
>
> And that's when he realized the casinos were the small game. **(L4 turns)**

## ACT 4: THE BIGGER TABLE (6:40–8:40)
> The biggest casino in the world doesn't have a roulette wheel. It has a ticker.

*Transition: a playing card flips into a stock certificate. 1960s trading floor.*

> Thorp saw that parts of the stock market were mispriced the way a blackjack deck gets mispriced. He'd buy what was too cheap, bet against what was too expensive, and hedge, so that whichever way the market moved, he'd come out ahead. **(L4 answered)**
>
> In 1967 he and economist Sheen Kassouf published ***Beat the Market.*** Their work on pricing options came six years before the famous Black–Scholes formula, the one that later won a Nobel Prize, and Thorp had been trading on similar ideas the whole time.
>
> Then he opened one of the first quantitative hedge funds. Nineteen years, around twenty percent a year, and not a single losing year.
>
> He'd beaten roulette, blackjack and Wall Street. But the smartest thing he ever did had nothing to do with winning. **(L6 planted)**

## ACT 5: THE TWIST (8:40–10:15)
> In 1991 a client asked him to look over their investments. One manager stood out: steady profits, month after month, never a bad year. Everyone trusted him. He was a pillar of Wall Street.
>
> Thorp did what he always did. He checked the numbers.

*Spreadsheet: 160 trades. Stamps land one by one.*

> He pulled about a hundred and sixty of the manager's options trades. For **half** of them, no such trade happened on the exchange that day. For many of the rest, the manager claimed more trades for just this one client than the whole market had traded that day.
>
> The trades weren't real. Thorp told his client to get out, and they did.
>
> The manager's name… was **Bernie Madoff.** **(L5 answered: twist)**

*Hard cut: December 2008 headlines.*

> Seventeen years later, in 2008, Madoff's operation collapsed. It was the largest Ponzi scheme in history. Regulators, banks and thousands of investors had looked at the same numbers and never checked them.
>
> Everyone remembers Ed Thorp as the man who beat the odds. But that isn't really his secret. **(L6 answered)** His secret was simpler, and harder: **when the numbers look too good to be true, check them.**

*Final shot echoes the cold open: the roulette ball, now slowing and stopping, and the soft tone in the ear.*

> The ball always stops somewhere. The question is whether you bothered to do the math.

*End card: sources in the description.*

## Verification notes (all checked 2026-09-29)
- **Roulette computer:** co-built with Claude Shannon in 1960–61. 12 transistors, cigarette-pack size, toe switches, eight tones for octants, +44% expected gain, tested in Las Vegas in summer 1961, and fragile wires meant no serious betting. Sources: Thorp, "The Invention of the First Wearable Computer" (1998), cs.virginia.edu/~evans/thorp.pdf; Winton; Engadget.
- **Blackjack:** backed by Manny Kimmel, a $10,000 bankroll turned into an $11,000 profit in one weekend in Reno. *Beat the Dealer* (1962). Rule changes, barring and disguises are all documented.
- **Drugged coffee and car accelerator:** **Thorp's own account** in *A Man for All Markets*, never proven, and he doesn't accuse anyone. The script says exactly this.
- **Beat the Market:** 1967, with Sheen Kassouf. Hedge ratio and dynamic hedging came six years before Black–Scholes (1973).
- **Princeton/Newport Partners:** 1969–1988, about 19–20% a year, no losing year (as reported).
- **Madoff:** in 1991 Thorp examined about 160 options trades. Half never occurred on the stated exchange, and for others the claimed volume exceeded the whole market. He advised the client to exit. Madoff was exposed in December 2008. Source: Thorp, "My Encounters with Madoff's Scheme and Other Swindles" (2009).
